# Angular Leafspot

## Description
Unlike most common strawberry leaf diseases, Angular Leafspot is caused by a bacterium, not a fungus. Because of this, traditional fungicide regimens are entirely ineffective against it. It causes localized tissue death and can heavily damage the calyx (the green cap of the strawberry), rendering the fruit unmarketable even if the berry itself remains uninfected.

## Symptoms
Early symptoms consist of tiny, water-soaked spots on the lower surface of leaves. Because the bacteria cannot easily cross the small veins in the leaf, the lesions take on a distinct blocky or "angular" shape. When held up to the light, these spots appear translucent (a "windowpane" effect); when viewed from above, they look dark green or black. Under wet conditions, a slimy, white bacterial exudate may ooze from the spots, which dries into a scaly film. The calyx may turn black and dry up.

## Causes / Pathogen
The disease is caused by the bacterium *Xanthomonas fragariae*. It is highly systemic, overwintering in dead leaves and plant debris.

## Conditions Favoring Disease
It favors cool to moderate daytime temperatures (65–70°F), cold nights (near freezing), and high relative humidity. It is heavily dependent on water for transmission; splashing rain or the use of overhead irrigation (especially for frost protection) are the primary drivers of field-wide spread. 

## Prevention
*   **Clean Stock:** The disease is usually introduced via infected nursery transplants. Always source certified disease-free plants.
*   **Site Management:** Avoid entering the field or moving equipment when plants are wet.
*   **Irrigation:** Limit overhead irrigation.
*   **Rotation:** Do not plant in soil containing undecomposed strawberry leaf debris from previous infected crops.

## Management
Since it is a systemic bacterium, angular leafspot cannot be eradicated once established. Copper-based bactericides can prevent the spread of the pathogen to healthy tissue, particularly the calyx, but must be used carefully—over-application (more than 4-5 sprays) causes phytotoxicity (reddening/burning of leaves). Plant defense activators like Actigard have shown some efficacy.

## Hydroponic Considerations
Indoor and hydroponic setups generally avoid Angular Leafspot unless infected transplants are brought into the system. Since indoor systems utilize drip irrigation, the "splashing water" vector is eliminated. If an outbreak occurs indoors, isolate and destroy the infected plants immediately and sterilize the system, as bacteria can easily spread through shared nutrient reservoirs or wet tools.
